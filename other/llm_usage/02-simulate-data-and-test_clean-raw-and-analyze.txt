❯ if we do anova analysis, are we assuming the occurence of warning days are    
  independent between beaches?                                                  
  ⎿  1 skill available

⏺ Yes — ANOVA assumes the residuals are independent, and that assumption is
  doubtful here in two ways.

  Across beaches on the same day. Rain and wind hit the whole city at once, so a
  wet week pushes every beach's E. coli up together. Your unit is a
  beach-month-year, and the month-year factor removes whatever is common to all
  beaches in that month, which is the main protection. But it only removes the
  monthly average shock. If July 2014 was wet in its first week and dry
  afterwards, all ten beaches still have correlated leftovers within that month,
  and those leftovers are the residuals.

  Within a beach over time. Question 2 is about exactly this: today's level
  partly carries over from yesterday. So a month's sampled days are not 30
  independent trials. A share of 0.2 from 30 correlated days carries less
  information than from 30 independent days.

  What goes wrong. Both kinds of correlation leave the residuals correlated but
  don't bias the beach averages themselves. The estimated beach differences stay
  roughly right; the estimated uncertainty is too small. The F test's
  denominator (the within-cell variance) understates the true variability, so
  p-values come out smaller than they should and Tukey's intervals come out too
  narrow. You would claim differences between beaches that the data doesn't
  really support.

  What to do about it.

  - Keep month-year in the model. It handles the largest part of the shared
    weather.
  - Check rather than assume: take the residuals, and for each pair of beaches
    correlate their residuals across month-years. If those correlations sit near
    0, the month-year factor did its job. If they're clearly positive, say so
    in the paper.
  - Your simulation is the honest test. It builds in a city-wide daily effect
    plus six beaches that are genuinely identical. Run the ANOVA and Tukey on
    simulated data over several seeds and count how often a pair of those six
    identical beaches is called different. If that rate sits near 5%, the method
    survives the correlation. If it's 20%, you've measured the problem and can
    report it.
  - If it is a problem, the standard fix is to let the errors be correlated
    within a month-year — a mixed model with a random effect for month-year, or
    cluster-robust standard errors clustered on month-year. Either widens the
    intervals to account for beaches moving together.

  The simulation check is worth adding to the plan's "What the analysis should
  recover" list, since right now that list assumes Tukey behaves. Want me to add
  it?

  Season start: Victoria Day, not June

  I compared each year's first sustained sampling day (first of three
  consecutive days with at least 8 beaches reporting) with Victoria Day and with
  1 June:

  ┌────────────┬─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │   Period   │                           Pattern                           │
  ├────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ 2007–2011  │ Starts late May to early June, 4–14 days after Victoria Day │
  ├────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ 2012–2026  │ Starts the day after Victoria Day in 11 of 15 years         │
  ├────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Exceptions │ 2017 (+7), 2021 (+8), 2025 (+3), and 2020 (+38, a late      │
  │            │ start at 15 June)                                           │
  └────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  Across all 20 years the median start is Victoria Day + 1 day, versus 8.5 days
  before 1 June. Since Victoria Day is a Monday, sampling begins the Tuesday
  after the holiday weekend — the City opens the beaches for the long weekend
  and starts testing the next working day.

  Cutting at 1 June would discard 7,284 results, about 7% of the record, and
  they aren't junk: from 2012 on, late May gives 8–13 sampling days a year at
  nearly every beach.

  Recommendation: keep the Victoria Day window. Two things to note in the paper:

  - Late May is cleaner than the rest of the season (8.6% of beach-days exceed
    100, against 13–16% in June through August), so including it lowers every
    beach's overall share a little. It shouldn't change the ranking, since it
    affects all beaches alike, but it's worth a sentence.
  - 2007 and 2008 have no late-May data at all, and 2020 starts in mid-June. The
    by-season shares handle this, since each season is summarized on its own
    days.

  Want me to settle that open decision in the plan and note the 8.6% figure?

  Questions on the new plan

  1. "Yesterday" in the warning-agreement count. The correlation section says
     previous sampling day, the agreement count doesn't say. When a beach has a
     gap (2009 missed 24 days mid-season; 2020-07-16 and 2026-07-17/18 have no
     results anywhere), do you want pairs only where the two days are genuinely
     consecutive, or any two successive sampling days? Consecutive-only is the
     honest version, since a warning posted from a sample three days ago isn't
     the process you're describing, and it costs few pairs.
  2. Sunnyside has 4 sites and Kew Balmy has 6. This affects Question 1 in a way
     the plan doesn't cover. The daily geometric mean of 4 samples is noisier
     than one of 6, and since exceedance means crossing a fixed threshold, extra

     daily mean from a fixed 4 randomly chosen sites (seeded) as a sensitivity
     check, or build unequal site counts into the simulation with equal
     baselines and measure the size of the effect. The second fits the
     simulation you already have.

⏺ Read the rubric. Here's what it asks for, and what that means for this script.

  What the rubric says

  The Tests row is scored 0, 2, 3, or 4 — there's no 1, so a weak attempt drops
  straight from "acceptable" to "poor". It asks for:

  - extensive suites for both the simulated and the real data
  - in separate scripts (stated twice)
  - built with a validation package — it names testthat, validate, pointblank,
    and Great Expectations

  The task list also says the simulation script itself should "develop some
  tests", so the pairing is: 00-simulate_data.py makes the data,
  01-test_simulated_data.py checks it, and a later script checks the cleaned
  real data with the same checks where they apply. That matches the plan and the
  starter folder's script names.

  Two other rubric rows touch this script: the reproducible-workflow row wants a
  preamble, comments, relative paths, a seed, and styled code; and anything you
  cite has to appear in the references.

  What the script should check

  Structure — the part that catches a broken pipeline:
  - the four columns exist, in order, with the right types (collectionDate a
    date, eColi an integer or null)
  - one row per site per day, no duplicates
  - 10 beaches, with the expected names spelled exactly
  - site counts per beach: 4 at Sunnyside, 6 at Kew Balmy, 5 elsewhere
  - every beach appears in every season
  - the row count equals days × sites, so nothing was silently dropped

  Values — the part that catches a broken simulation:
  - every date falls between Victoria Day and Labour Day of its own year
  - all 20 seasons present, 2007 to 2026
  - results are 10 or more and are multiples of 10
  - no absurd values (the real data's one 6-million reading is the thing being
    excluded)
  - the share at the detection limit is near half
  - the blank share sits in a plausible range

  Structure the simulation builds in — this is what lifts the suite past a
  column-checker, and it's the part a marker notices:
  - blanks are mostly whole beach-days, not scattered samples
  - every beach has at least 4 results on most days, so the geometric-mean rule
    has data to work with
  - consecutive days correlate positively, since carry-over was built in
  - the two high beaches exceed the threshold more often than the common ones,
    in the built-in order
  - exceedance days per beach-season vary more than a Poisson distribution
    predicts

  That last group is worth doing because the tests then check the design, not
  just the file. If someone changes a spread and the data stops looking like
  Toronto's, a test fails and says which assumption broke.

  A caution on those: they're statistical, so a threshold set too tight will
  fail on some seeds. I'd write them against generous bounds (correlation above
  0.2, not equal to 0.43) and state in a comment that the bound is loose on
  purpose.

❯ one of the requirements for the paper is to show all the data. how about a    
  violin plot for each beach?                                                   

⏺ A violin would be the wrong tool here, for three reasons specific to this
  data.

  The floor breaks it. 49% of results are exactly 10. A violin is a kernel
  density estimate, which smooths — it would draw that spike as a fat smooth
  bulge and, worse, put density below 10 where no value can exist. It would also
  smear the gaps between the reporting steps (10, 20, 30 …) into a continuum
  the data doesn't have. The bandwidth choice, which you'd never show the
  reader, would control how the picture looks.

  It's a summary, not the data. The requirement is that a graph shows the actual
  observations, or as close as possible, and boxplots are singled out as
  needing the real data overlaid. A violin is in the same family: it shows a
  smoothed density estimate, not 99,000 measurements. Whatever passes for a
  boxplot would apply to a violin too.

  It hides the thing you care about. Your question is about days above 100. A
  violin's widest part sits at the floor, where nothing interesting happens, and
  the tail above 100 — the part that matters — is the thinnest, least legible
  sliver.

  What I'd do instead

  A histogram of every result, faceted by beach, on a log10 x-axis. The FAQ
  explicitly counts a histogram as showing the data, as long as the bars add up
  to the number of observations. It's the honest form here because the data is
  already discrete: values come in steps of 10, so the bars are the data rather
  than a smoothing of it. The floor shows up as what it is, a single tall bar at
  10, and a vertical line at 100 lets the reader see each beach's exceedance
  mass directly. Ten small panels sharing an axis make the beaches comparable at
  a glance.

  Two things it needs: a note that counts differ between beaches (Sunnyside has
  4 sites, Kew Balmy 6), and the y-axis free or fixed depending on whether you
  want to compare shapes or volumes. I'd fix it, so a taller panel means more
  sampling.

  Then a second graph for the beach-day geometric means, since that's the unit
  the analysis actually uses — about 20,000 points rather than 99,000. A
  jittered strip plot works there: one point per beach-day, low opacity, with
  the threshold line. That one genuinely plots every observation.

  You likely want a third showing the 6,191,768 reading before it's removed,
  with the removal explained in the caption.

  If you want the violin shape anyway, the defensible version is a jittered
  strip plot of the real points with a violin outline behind it — but once the
  points are there, the outline adds little.

  Want me to build the faceted histogram first, against the simulated data so
  the plotting code is ready before the real data is cleaned?

⏺ The figure is saved as other/explore/figures/all-raw-results.png, and it
  caught something worth knowing before cleaning.

  There's an upper reporting limit too, and it changed over time.

  ┌───────────┬───────────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────┐
  │  Period   │ Results reported as exactly 1,000 │ Results above 1,000 │
  ├───────────┼───────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────┤
  │ 2007–2017 │ 0 to 0.2%                         │ 39 to 133 a year    │
  ├───────────┼───────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────┤
  │ 2018–2025 │ 1.0% to 2.3%                      │ 10 to 42 a year     │
  ├───────────┼───────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────┤
  │ 2026      │ 1.8%                              │ zero                │
  └───────────┴───────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────┘

  Before 2018, exactly 1,000 was a value like any other and readings ran up to
  15,820. From 2018 the lab starts piling results at exactly 1,000, and in 2026
  nothing above 1,000 is reported at all, out of 5,684 results. That's a
  ceiling: high samples are being reported as "1,000" rather than measured.

  So the data is censored at both ends — 10 at the bottom, 1,000 at the top —
  and the top one arrived partway through the record.

  What it means for the paper:

  - The main result is untouched. A censored value of 1,000 is ten times the
    threshold, so a day capped at 1,000 was over 100 either way. Counting days
    above 100 doesn't care whether the true value was 1,000 or 15,000.
  - The model is mildly affected. Beach coefficients come from mean log10
    values, and capping the top squeezes those means slightly toward zero, more
    so after 2018. It touches 1–2% of samples, so the effect is small, but a
    beach with more high days loses more.
  - Don't drop or adjust them. Dropping capped values would throw away the worst
    days, which is the opposite of what you want. Documenting the ceiling and
    noting the direction of the bias is the honest handling.

  This also gives the floor sensitivity check a natural partner: the plan
  already recomputes with the floor at 5, and the same paragraph can report the
  headline numbers with capped values excluded, to show the conclusions don't
  hinge on them.

  writing scripts/03-clean_data.py?

❯ is there an auto line break in the py file viewer?                            

⏺ Two possible meanings, so both:

  In PyCharm (the editor): yes, it's called soft wrap and it's off by default
  for code. Turn it on by right-clicking the left gutter → Soft-Wrap, or
  globally at Settings → Editor → General → Soft Wraps, where the "Soft-wrap
  these files" mask defaults to *.md; *.txt; *.rst — add *.py to include Python.
  It only changes the display; no characters are written to the file, so the
  long caption strings stay one line on disk.

  In matplotlib (the figure): no automatic wrapping by default, which is why the
  caption carries explicit \n breaks. Text does accept wrap=True, which wraps
  at the figure edge:

  figure.text(0.008, 0.955, "long caption …", wrap=True, ...)

  I didn't use it here because it wraps to the figure width rather than a width
  you choose, and it ignores bbox_inches="tight" when the figure is saved — so
  the result often differs between what you see and what lands in the PNG.
  Manual breaks are predictable.

  If you'd rather not hand-place the breaks, the middle option is textwrap.fill,
  which the script already imports for the panel titles:

  figure.text(0.008, 0.955, fill(caption_text, width=150), ...)

  That gives you one number to tune instead of deciding where each break goes.

❯ what do you mean staged and unstaged?                                         

⏺ Git has three places a change can live, and git status --short prints two
  columns — left column = staging area, right column = working directory.

  D  other/explore/figures/all-beach-days.png       ← D in left column: deletion
  is staged
  D  other/explore/figures/all-results-by-beach.png ← staged
   M scripts/05-exploratory_data_analysis.py        ← M in right column:
  modified, not staged

  Working directory is your actual files on disk. Staging area (the "index") is
  the set of changes you've marked to go into the next commit. The commit is
  what gets recorded permanently.

  The split here happened because of how the two changes were made:

  - I used git rm, which deletes the file and stages the deletion in one step —
    so those show in the left column.
  - I edited the script with a normal write, which only touches the working
    directory — so it shows in the right column, unstaged.

  It makes no practical difference to you, because the way we've been committing
  is git add -A, which stages everything before committing. The result is
  identical either way.

  The distinction matters when you want to commit only some of your changes —
  stage those files, leave the rest, commit. That's also why git commit -a
  exists: it stages all modified tracked files and commits in one go.

  So: nothing is committed yet. Both the deletions and the script edit are
  waiting, and one git add -A && git commit will take all three.

⏺ For all-beach-days.png, three numbers, all in the same loop:

  - scripts/05-exploratory_data_analysis.py:377 — s=3, the dot size for both
    colours. It's in points squared, so 12 makes a dot twice as wide, not twice
    as big.
  - scripts/05-exploratory_data_analysis.py:371 — 0.12, the opacity of the blue
    points.
  - scripts/05-exploratory_data_analysis.py:372 — 0.35, the opacity of the
    orange ones.

  The two opacities differ on purpose: there are far fewer orange points, so at
  matching alpha they'd look fainter than the blue mass. If you raise one, raise
  the other by roughly the same ratio.

  The other figures, for reference:

  ┌──────────────────────────────────┬──────────┬──────────┐
  │              Figure              │   Size   │  Alpha   │
  ├──────────────────────────────────┼──────────┼──────────┤
  │ all-raw-results.png, samples     │ line 113 │ line 114 │
  ├──────────────────────────────────┼──────────┼──────────┤
  │ all-raw-results.png, outlier dot │ line 126 │ opaque   │
  ├──────────────────────────────────┼──────────┼──────────┤
  │ all-raw-results.png, red ring    │ line 134 │ opaque   │
  └──────────────────────────────────┴──────────┴──────────┘

  The rule of thumb when tuning: size and alpha trade off against each other.
  Bigger dots with the same alpha darken the dense regions and hide structure;
  the usual fix is to raise size and lower alpha together, so the dense areas
  stay readable while isolated points get easier to see. At 19,662 points in ten
  rows, something like s=6 with 0.08/0.25 is worth trying if you want the
  sparse right-hand tail more visible.

  Marie Curtis and Sunnyside each sit 0.34 above the daily average on the log10
  scale — more than twice the E. coli of the average beach on the same day.
  They're statistically indistinguishable from each other (p = 0.99) and clearly
  above everything else. 32 of the 45 pairwise comparisons separate after
  correction.

  It holds up to every check I threw at it. Across seasons, Marie Curtis ranges
  22–60% and Sunnyside 21–47%, while the cleanest beaches never pass 12% in any
  single season — the two groups never overlap. Splitting the record at 2018,
  when the reporting ceiling appeared, barely moves anything. And the simulation
  says censoring shrinks these gaps by about a third, so the true differences
  are larger than reported.

  Finding 2: yesterday's result is close to a coin toss

  Results take ~24 hours, so a posting describes yesterday's water. Over 18,998
  consecutive-day pairs:

  - Of days actually over the limit, 63% were missed — 1,700 of 2,702.
  - Of warnings that would be posted, 63% were unnecessary — 1,712 of 2,714.

  One day's reading explains about 14% of the variation in the next (correlation
  0.372 excluding pairs where both days sat at the floor). Water quality
  changes faster than the testing follows.

  The story for the paper

  Which beach you choose matters far more than which day. Marie Curtis Park East
  is over the limit roughly eight times as often as Gibraltar Point,
  consistently, for twenty years — while the day-to-day signal that drives
  actual postings is barely better than chance.

  Three caveats

  1. These are days over Toronto's limit as computed here, not recorded closures
     — the data doesn't record postings, and the City may use a two-day mean.
  2. The estimated gaps are floors, because of the reporting floor and ceiling.
  3. Days over the limit cluster hard — variance 8.7× the mean, against 1 for
     Poisson — which is why the standard errors are clustered by month. Treating
     days as independent would have badly overstated the precision.
