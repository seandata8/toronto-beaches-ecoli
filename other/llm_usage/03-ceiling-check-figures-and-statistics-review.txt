⏺ No. Of the 290 beach-days since 2018 with at least one reading of exactly
  1,000, 256 had a geomean above 100 and 34 did not (all 34 below 100, none
  exactly 100). Every one of the 34 had a single 1,000 reading. The other
  samples were low enough to keep the geomean under 100.

  Could the cap have hidden a warning? Raising the capped reading can only raise
  the geomean. For each day I worked out how high the true value would have had
  to be to push the geomean over 100: x = 1000 × (100/geomean)^n, where n is
  the number of samples that day.

  ┌────────────────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────────┐
  │   True value needed to reach a     │             Beach-days             │

  ├────────────────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────┤

  ├────────────────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ > 20,000                           │ 9 (the cap almost certainly didn't │
  │                                    │  matter)                           │
  └────────────────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────────────┘

  The closest calls:
  - Centre Island 2024-08-12: a true value of about 1,130 would have been
    enough.
  - Centre Island 2025-08-22: about 1,230.
  - Marie Curtis 2023-08-25: about 1,490.
  - Bluffer's 2020-06-15 and -16, and Centre Island 2022-07-29: about 1,670.

  Centre Island accounts for 13 of the 34.

  So yes, the cap could have hidden warnings, but only on a handful of days. For
  about 8–18 of these days, a plausible true value (under 2,000–5,000) would
  have put the geomean over 100. Since the City's posting decision rests on the
  geomean, a lab report capped at 1,000 wouldn't just shrink the reported
  number; it could hide a posting.

  Two things to check before this goes in the paper:
  1. The cap isn't total. Values above 1,000 still show up every year from 2018
     to 2025 (10–42 a year, up to 12,000). Only 2026 has none. So 1,000 may be a
     ceiling on some samples (perhaps one dilution) rather than a hard limit,
     and some of these 1,000s may be real. That's a question worth adding to the
     one you're sending the City.
  2. Bluffer's 2020-06-15 and 2020-06-16 have identical values (20, 50, 50,
     120, 1000). That looks like a duplicated day, not two real samplings.

  So "Marie Curtis was over the limit on 34.1% of its days" is simply a fact
  about 2007–2026. There's no sampling error in it, and a test is no help in
  describing it.

  beach, as a lasting property of the place, not just that it had bad luck.
  That's where chance comes in. The 20 summers we got had one particular pattern
  of rainstorms, heat waves and winds. Twenty different summers at the same
  beaches would have given somewhat different shares, say 31% or 37% instead of
  34%.

  In lab terms, the 20 summers are one run of an experiment. The test asks
  whether the gap between two beaches is bigger than the scatter you'd expect if
  you repeated the run. The standard error estimates that scatter between runs.
  A difference counts as "significant" when it's several standard errors wide,
  so another run would be unlikely to flip the order.

  For Marie Curtis against Gibraltar Point (34% against 4%), the gap is far

  pairs aren't significant.

  independent repeats. One storm can push a beach over the limit for four days

⏺ Yes. 32 of the 45 pairs are significant and 13 aren't, at the 5% level after
  correcting for making 45 comparisons. The method isn't quite a paired t-test
  with Bonferroni, though. It's a close relative of both.

  The test: a regression that works like a paired comparison
  - Each beach-day's value is the log10 geometric mean minus that day's average
    across all beaches sampled that day. Beaches are only ever compared with
    each other on the same day, so a rainy week that raises every beach cancels
    out. That's the "paired" idea, applied to ten beaches at once.
  - A regression of that value on beach gives each beach's average gap from the
    others. Each pair of beaches is then compared with a t-test on the
    difference between their gaps.
  - The standard errors are calculated by calendar month, e.g. July 2019 (96
    months in total), not by day, because consecutive days aren't independent.
    An ordinary paired t-test on daily differences would treat every day as
    independent evidence and give p-values that are far too small.

  The correction: Holm, not Bonferroni
  - Bonferroni tests every pair against 0.05/45 = 0.0011.
  - Holm sorts the p-values from smallest to largest and tests them against
    0.05/45, then 0.05/44, 0.05/43, and so on, stopping at the first one that
    fails. It keeps the same guarantee (about a 5% chance of any false
    difference across all 45 pairs) but loses less power.
  - It matters for exactly one pair here. Bluffer's against Cherry has a raw p
    of 0.0023, which is significant under Holm (corrected p = 0.033) but not
    under Bonferroni. So Bonferroni would give 31 significant pairs instead
    of 32.

⏺ Yes, 45 pairs: ten beaches taken two at a time is 10 × 9 / 2 = 45.

  Visualising them

  I wouldn't plot the p-values themselves. They range from about 10⁻¹⁴⁸ to 1, so
  on any readable scale the plot just says "the two worst beaches are very
  different" and squashes everything else. A p-value also mixes together how big
  a difference is and how precisely it's measured, and readers mostly want the
  first. Two better options:

  1. Each beach's estimate with a 95% confidence interval (my pick for the
     paper). This is a dot per beach with an error bar, sorted from dirtiest to
     cleanest, on a scale like "times the average beach on the same day". Where
     two error bars clearly don't overlap, those beaches differ. Where they
     overlap a lot, the beaches can't be told apart. It's one small figure, a
     general reader can follow it, and it shows effect size and uncertainty
     together. One caveat: overlap is only a rough guide. The exact answer is in
     the pairwise tests, which can go in an appendix.
  2. A 10 × 10 grid of pairs (lower triangle only, so 45 cells). Each cell is
     coloured by how much higher one beach runs than the other, and the 13
     non-significant cells are greyed out or marked. It's complete, but it's
     heavier going. It suits an appendix if you want every pair shown.

  Effect size: yes, and it matters more than the p-value here

  The model's differences are on the log10 scale, so the natural effect size is
  a fold difference, 10^(difference). It's the same idea as a concentration
  ratio:

  Why it matters:

  - With 19,662 beach-days, small differences can still be significant. A 15%
    difference between Gibraltar Point and Ward's Island is statistically real,
    but it probably doesn't matter to a swimmer. The finding worth reporting is
    the 2–3× gap between the two worst beaches and the rest. The significance
    test only confirms it isn't luck.
  - Significance depends on precision, not just size. Gibraltar–Ward's (1.15×)
    is significant and Gibraltar–Woodbine (1.14×) isn't, because Woodbine's
    daily values bounce around more, which widens its standard error. Two nearly

  - These fold differences are lower bounds. The simulation showed the reporting
    floor at 10 and ceiling at 1,000 shrink the gaps to about 65% of their true
    size, so the real 2.2× is probably nearer 3×.

  share of days over the limit, e.g. 34% at Marie Curtis against 4% at Gibraltar
  Point, 30 percentage points. The paper can lead with that and use the model's
  fold differences as backup.

⏺ Here's the draft figure: paper/figures/beach-estimates.png, made by a new
  section at the end of scripts/07-analyse_data.py. I haven't committed it.

  How to read it:
  - The dot shows how high a beach's E. coli runs compared with the average
    beach sampled the same day. For example, 2.19× means a little over twice as
    high.
  - The bar is the 95% confidence interval.
  - The dashed line at 1× means the same as the average beach that day.
  - The scale is logarithmic, so "half as high" and "twice as high" sit the same
    distance from the line.
  - The numbers on the right are the estimates, placed in a column so they can't
    collide with the line.

  What it shows:
  - Sunnyside and Marie Curtis sit at 2.19× with intervals far from everyone
    else, and on top of each other. That matches "they differ from all others
    but not from each other".
  - The other eight form a loose group from 0.66× to 1.22×, with overlapping
    intervals among the lower beaches. That's where the 13 non-significant pairs
    are.

  One thing you'll notice: Centre Island (1.22×) ranks above Kew Balmy (1.09×)
  here. In the share of days over the limit, Kew Balmy ranks higher (15.7%
  against 13.9%). The two measures answer different questions:
  - The model compares a beach's typical level on the same days.
  - The share counts how often a beach crosses 100.

  A beach with more days at both extremes (many at the floor of 10, a few very
  high) can cross 100 more often while having a lower average. The difference is

⏺ What the check does

  1. Count days over the limit for each beach in each year. That gives 200
     counts (10 beaches × 20 years). For example, Marie Curtis averages about 33
     days a year and Gibraltar Point about 4.
  2. Compare how spread out those counts are with their average. If over-limit
     days happened independently, like radioactive decays, where each event is
     unrelated to the last, the counts would follow a Poisson distribution. A
     Poisson distribution has a variance equal to its mean, so the variance
     divided by the mean would be about 1.
  3. Result: 8.7. The counts are far more spread out than independent days would
     give. The notes read that as "over-limit days come in runs": one storm
     puts a beach over for several days in a row, so some years pile up many and
     others few.

  The problem: 8.7 mixes three things

  I split it by beach to check, and most of the 8.7 isn't clustering at all:

  - Differences between beaches, most of it. Pooling all 200 counts mixes a
    beach averaging 33 days with one averaging 4. That alone makes the spread
    large, even if every beach were perfectly Poisson. Within each beach, the
    ratio is only 1.2 to 4.8:

  | Beach           | Mean days over limit per year | Variance ÷ mean |
  |-----------------|-------------------------------|-----------------|
  | Marie Curtis    | 32.9                          | 2.3             |
  | Sunnyside       | 31.6                          | 1.2             |
  | Centre Island   | 13.4                          | 4.3             |
  | Bluffer's       | 11.3                          | 4.8             |
  | Gibraltar Point | 4.3                           | 1.3             |

  - Wet years against dry years. Even within one beach, some summers are rainier

⏺ What a standard error is

  A standard error says how much an estimate would wobble if you repeated the
  whole data collection. The textbook formula is

    SE = (spread of the individual values) ÷ √n

  where n is the number of independent observations. More independent data gives
  a smaller SE, but only in proportion to √n.

  Without grouping (treating every day as independent)

  Each beach has about 1,950 beach-days. Plugging n ≈ 1,950 into the formula
  assumes each day is new, independent information, like 1,950 separate
  experiments.

  With grouping by month

  Days aren't independent: a wet spell raises a beach for several days running.
  So 1,950 days hold less information than 1,950 independent experiments would.
  It's like injecting the same sample five times: you get five numbers, but not
  five independent measurements of the sample.

  The grouped (clustered) method doesn't assume independence within a month. It
  works out the uncertainty from how much the 96 months differ from each other,
  so a run of related days counts as roughly one piece of evidence, not several.
  The result is a larger, more honest SE.

  The 1.9×

  I fitted the same model both ways and compared the SEs for each beach:

  ┌─────────────────────┬─────────────────────────┬─────────────────┬───────┐
  │        Beach        │  SE, days treated as    │ SE, grouped by  │ Ratio │
  │                     │       independent       │      month      │       │
  ├─────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┼─────────────────┼───────┤
  │ Marie Curtis        │ 0.008                   │ 0.019           │ 2.25  │
  ├─────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┼─────────────────┼───────┤
  │ Gibraltar Point     │ 0.008                   │ 0.012           │ 1.39  │
  ├─────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┼─────────────────┼───────┤
  │ Median over the 10  │                         │                 │ 1.9   │
  │ beaches             │                         │                 │       │
  └─────────────────────┴─────────────────────────┴─────────────────┴───────┘

  The estimates themselves are identical in both fits. Only the error bars
  change.

  What 1.9× means in practice

  - The 95% intervals are about twice as wide as they'd be if days were treated
    as independent. The intervals in the beach-estimates figure are the wider,
    correct ones.
  - The effective sample size is much smaller. Because the SE shrinks with √n,
    an SE 1.9 times larger means the data carry about as much information as
    1/1.9² ≈ 1/3.6 as many independent days. So roughly 540 independent days per
    beach instead of 1,950.
  - Without the grouping, p-values would be far too small and more pairs of
    beaches would look significantly different than really are.

  That's why the ratio is direct evidence for the grouping. If days were really
  independent, the ratio would come out near 1 and the grouping wouldn't change
  anything. At 1.3–2.3, it clearly does.

  The catch with accuracy

  Most days are clean (86%), so a rule that never posts a warning would be right
  86% of the time. That beats the warning system's 82%. That doesn't make
  warnings useless: the never-warn rule catches zero dirty days. It shows why
  accuracy is the wrong measure when one outcome is rare. The measures that
  matter are the ones above: warnings catch about a third of dirty days, and
  about a third of warnings are right.

  is dirty 37% of the time. A day with an all-clear is dirty 10% of the time
  (1,700 of 16,284), and a random day 14%. So a warning raises the chance of
  dirty water about 3.5-fold. It carries information, but not much.

  samples catches about a third of the days that are over the limit, and about a
  third of such warnings are needed." I'd skip the accuracy figure, or mention
  it only to explain why it misleads.
