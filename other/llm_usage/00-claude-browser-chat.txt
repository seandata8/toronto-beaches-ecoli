[Claude's reasoning: Explaining why simulating data first matters in a workflow.]
It feels backwards, but the order is deliberate. Simulating first makes you write down what you *expect* the data to look like before real values can shape your thinking. That pays off in three ways.

It gives you tests with teeth. Your expectations — E. coli counts are never negative, samples fall within the swimming season, beach names come from a fixed list, each beach is sampled roughly daily — become tests. When you run those against the real data, anything that fails is a surprise worth investigating: a data-entry error, a changed collection practice, or something genuinely interesting.

It lets you check your code against a known answer. If you simulate higher counts after heavy rain and your graphs and summaries show exactly that, you know your code works. With real data alone, you can't tell a real pattern from a bug.

It keeps you honest about the story. Deciding what you're looking for before seeing the numbers makes it harder to wander through the data until something happens to look striking.

You're right that you shouldn't simulate blind, though. Reading the dataset's description, column names, and collection method first is fine and sensible — that tells you the structure. The point is to simulate before you look at the actual values.

For a descriptive paper like this one, the biggest payoff is the tests. And both the simulation and the tests are graded items.

Strictly, the beach isn't closed — Toronto Public Health posts a warning against swimming, and lifeguards stay on duty.

The multiple readings are combined into a single number using a geometric mean. Toronto Public Health issues beach advisories when the geometric mean concentration of at least five samples exceeds 100 E. coli / 100 mL of water. A peer-reviewed study of Toronto beaches adds a detail the City's pages don't mention: the city is unique in using a 2-day rolling geometric mean of 100 E. coli CFU/100 mL for beach posting decisions, meaning the average draws on samples from the two most recent sampling days rather than one.

A geometric mean is an average taken on a multiplicative scale rather than an additive one. Its practical effect is that one very high reading raises it far less than it would raise an ordinary average. That suits bacteria counts, which often sit in the tens but occasionally spike into the thousands at a single sampling point.

Toronto's threshold is stricter than the national one. Canadian beaches are posted to advise against swimming if the geometric mean exceeds 200 E. coli CFU/100 mL (Health Canada, 2012), but some jurisdictions (e.g., Toronto Public Health) continue to use a more stringent geometric mean of 100. The federal guideline also includes a single-sample trigger: water is considered unsuitable if the geometric mean of at least five samples exceeds 200 E. coli per 100 mL or if a single sample exceeds 400.

Because culture results take about a day, posting decisions are delayed about 24 h and are based on previous-day water quality conditions. The same study found that there was no significant correlation between previous-day and same-day E. coli results at any Sunnyside Beach sampling transect — yesterday's water was a poor guide to today's. Your plan already has the key insight right: the shared weather affects the *uncertainty*, not the *point estimates*. It's worth being precise about why, because it changes what you need to defend.

**What you're estimating.** Each beach has a long-run tendency to exceed 100, and you're estimating that tendency from a finite run of days. Rain isn't a confounder for this question. A confounder would be something that makes beach A *look* worse than beach B when it isn't — but rain hits both on the same days, and averaged over twenty seasons each beach experiences essentially the same weather. If river-mouth beaches respond more strongly to rain, that responsiveness is part of what makes them worse, not a distortion of it. So the ranking is not at risk. What's at risk is the honesty of your error bars.

**Two kinds of dependence, and they do different things.** Across beaches on the same day, a wet week pushes everyone up together. Within a beach across days, today resembles yesterday. Both mean your thousands of beach-days contain far less information than thousands of independent observations would. The effective sample size might be a few hundred. Standard methods assuming independence will report intervals that are too narrow and p-values too small — which is exactly what your simulation check is designed to detect. That's a good design.

**Ten beaches is a population, not a sample.** You have all of Toronto's beaches, so you're not generalizing to unobserved beaches. The only thing chance enters through is which weather these twenty summers happened to bring. Framing your uncertainty that way — "would a different run of summers have reordered these beaches?" — is more honest than the usual sampling story, and it makes clear why clustering by time is the thing to get right.

**On the paired test: yes, it's valid, and it's better-motivated than your ANOVA.** Pairing on the same day is blocking, and it removes the city-wide weather effect exactly rather than approximately — your month-year factor only removes each month's average, leaving within-month weather in the residuals. Pair the two beaches' log₁₀ daily geometric means on days when both were sampled, and test whether the mean difference is zero. On the log scale that difference is a log ratio, so you're asking whether one beach runs consistently higher than the other by some multiplicative factor, which is a natural thing to report.

Three things to handle:

Consecutive days are still correlated, so the paired t-test's standard error is too small for the same reason as before. Cluster by month-year, or block-bootstrap whole seasons, or thin to one day per week and check whether the conclusion survives.

With ten beaches there are 45 pairs, so correct for multiplicity — Holm or Tukey on the paired differences.

The reporting floor is the awkward one. If about half your values are 10, then on days when both beaches are at the floor the difference is exactly zero — not because the beaches are identical but because the measurement can't go lower. [Claude's reasoning: Examining whether warning-day frequency fits a Poisson model. / Comparing binomial and negative binomial alternatives to Poisson.]
Small correction first: the floor at 10 comes from the lab, not from the geometric mean. If they filter 10 mL, one colony is the smallest thing that can be seen, and that registers as 10 per 100 mL. The zero problem is a consequence — a plate with no colonies has to be recorded as something, and 10 is the conventional choice.

On Poisson: I doubt it fits, for two reasons.

**It's really a binomial situation.** Poisson describes counts with no natural ceiling — events arriving in continuous time. Your warning days are a count *out of* a known number of sampled days, so each sampled day is a yes/no trial and the natural description is binomial. Poisson approximates binomial when the probability is small, which is fine for a beach posted 4% of the time and quite wrong for one posted 40% of the time. Since the beaches you care about most are the frequently-posted ones, that approximation fails exactly where it matters.

**Warning days cluster.** Both distributions assume trials are independent at a constant rate. A wet week posts a beach for several days running, so warning days arrive in clumps rather than scattered. The consequence is overdispersion: the count varies more across seasons than either distribution predicts. Fitting Poisson anyway gives roughly the right average rate but standard errors that are too small — the same failure mode as your ANOVA concern, in different clothing.

If you did want to model it, the usual ladder is logistic regression for the per-day yes/no outcome, then something that allows extra variability — a random effect for beach-season, or standard errors clustered by month-year. Negative binomial is the count-based equivalent and is often described as Poisson with an extra parameter for the excess variability.

And yes, this is exactly what "choosing a model" means. You're asserting a mechanism that generated the numbers, and that assertion has testable consequences. Which is the useful part: you can check it cheaply. Count warning days per beach per season, then compare the variance across seasons to the mean. Poisson predicts they're roughly equal; if the variance is several times the mean, clustering is real and Poisson is out. Your simulation is even better for this, since you built in day-to-day carry-over and know what the answer should be — run the same check there and you'll see the dispersion inflate as you raise the carry-over fraction.
